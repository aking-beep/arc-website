# Module 08 · Launch

**The decision.** Which single channel are you going to work, what is the
price and why, and which two numbers decide whether you continue?

**Why it is cheaper here.** Distribution is the assumption most often left
untested, because it is the only one you cannot satisfy by building something.
Access was one of the four assumption types in module 01, and if it was never
the circled one, this is where it collects. A product nobody can find performs
exactly like a product that does not work, and takes longer to diagnose.

## The work

### Pick one channel and work it properly

One. Listing on six platforms in launch week is not six channels, it is one
weak attempt divided six ways, and none of them produces enough signal to
learn from.

Choose on two criteria: can you reach the specific person from slot one of your
hypothesis, and can you sustain it for three months? Most channels fail on the
second. A channel is a habit, not an event.

| Channel | Works when | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing in public | The problem is one people search for or argue about | You stop after four posts |
| Community participation | A real community exists and you are already in it | You arrive to promote and leave |
| Direct outreach | The customer is identifiable and the price justifies the effort | The list is bought rather than researched |
| Launch platforms | The product is self-serve and demos in a screenshot | It is the only thing you do |
| Partnerships | Someone already sells to this exact person | You need them more than they need you |

Write down what sustaining it looks like in a normal week — hours, and what
you produce. If that sentence is not realistic alongside building, the channel
is aspirational.

### Set a price you can defend

Three inputs, and you already have two of them. The cost of the alternative
from module 02. The cost per user from module 07. And the shape of the
decision: whether this is an individual spending their own money, an expense
nobody questions, or a procurement process with a security review attached.

Price against the alternative's cost, not against your costs. Your costs set
the floor below which you should not go; they say nothing about what the thing
is worth to the buyer.

Two mistakes, in order of frequency. Pricing low to reduce objections, which
removes your margin and signals that the product is marginal — low prices do
not make an unwanted product wanted. And pricing into a procurement process you
cannot survive, where a $2,000 annual contract triggers a review cycle that
costs you more than the contract.

Write one sentence of reasoning next to the number. If you cannot, you guessed,
and you will discount at the first objection.

### Name the two numbers before launch day

Launch produces a flood of ambiguous data. Decide beforehand which two numbers
you are reading, and what each one would have to say to change the plan.

Usually one is activation — the share of people who arrive and complete the
workflow once — and one is return, which is the only real evidence of value.
Vanity metrics are the ones that can only go up: signups, page views, mentions.

Set a review date and the decision rule:

> Four weeks from launch. If activation is under 20% the problem is the
> product or the promise, and we go back to module 03. If activation is fine
> and return is under 15%, the problem is value and we go back to module 02.
> If both hold, we double the channel.

### Decide how feedback reaches you

The default is that it does not. Nobody files a complaint about a product they
tried once; they leave.

Pick a mechanism that does not depend on the user being motivated: a prompt at
the moment of rejection, a short email to the first fifty users, the override
rate from module 07. The users you most need to hear from are the ones who
already left, so instrument the exit rather than waiting for the inbox.

### Plan the first iteration before you need it

Write what you expect to be wrong. Most teams are right about this and do it
anyway, because the alternative — deciding under pressure in week two — is
worse.

If activation fails, the first thing you change is the promise or the first
run, not the feature set. Adding features to a product people do not activate
on makes the product bigger and the problem unchanged.

## You should be holding

- [ ] One channel, with a realistic weekly commitment written down
- [ ] A price, with one sentence of reasoning beside it
- [ ] Two numbers, with thresholds and a dated review
- [ ] A feedback mechanism that does not rely on user motivation
- [ ] A written expectation of what will be wrong first

## Where this bites

**The symptom:** launch day was the plan. Launch is a day; distribution is a
habit. The products that work are usually the ones still doing the boring
channel in month four.

**The second symptom:** the numbers all went up and you cannot tell if it
worked. Everything goes up on launch day. Only the numbers you named in
advance, measured at the date you set, mean anything.

**The third symptom:** you are adding features in response to low activation.
If people are not completing the workflow once, more workflows will not help.
Go back to module 03 and check that the one workflow is actually one workflow.
