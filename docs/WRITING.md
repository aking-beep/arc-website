# How ARC writes

The standard for every word on the site, in a proposal, or in a product.

The tension this document exists to manage: a CEO with limited technical
knowledge has to understand it, and a CTO has to still believe we know what we
are doing. Simplifying usually breaks the second one. Sounding credible usually
breaks the first. Both failures are easy; holding both is the job.

## The ten tests

Every piece of copy has to pass all ten.

1. **Could a customer say this sentence?** If you cannot imagine a buyer saying
   it out loud to a colleague, it is our language, not theirs.
2. **Does it name a problem or an outcome before a technology?** The technology
   is how we solve it. It is not why anyone is reading.
3. **Does removing "AI", "innovative", "transformation", "strategic" or
   "scalable" leave the sentence meaningless?** If the word is doing the work,
   the sentence has no content. Rewrite it.
4. **Is there a concrete noun or a specific action in it?** "Dashboards",
   "releases", "invoices", "a login" beat "capabilities", "solutions",
   "outcomes".
5. **Are three sentences doing the work of one?** Cut two.
6. **Does the page say who it is for?** A page that is for everyone converts
   nobody.
7. **Does it show evidence rather than claim credibility?** A number, a named
   client, a before and after. Claiming to be rigorous is not evidence of rigour.
8. **Does the call to action describe something a human wants to do?**
   "Tell us what is not working" beats "Request a consultation".
9. **Would a CEO with limited technical knowledge understand it?**
10. **Would a CTO still believe we know what we are doing?**

## Retired constructions

These are the fingerprints of machine-written business copy. We used them often
enough that the effort to sound unlike AI started to read like AI. They are out
unless a specific sentence genuinely needs one.

| Pattern | Example of the problem | Write instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `X, not Y` | "Evidence, not claims." | Say the thing. "We show you what changed in the system that was supposed to change." |
| `real X` | "real systems", "real constraints", "the real number" | Name the system, the constraint, the number. |
| `actually` | "what actually works", "who actually does the work" | Delete it. If the sentence collapses, it had no content. |
| `honest X` | "an honest read", "honest proof" | Show the thing that makes it honest. Say what you do not know. |
| `not another X` | "Not another strategy deck." | Describe what it is. |
| `X that ships` | "strategy that ships" | Say what arrives and when. |
| `no buzzwords` / `plain language` | Announcing the register instead of using it | Just write plainly. Saying you are plain-spoken is not. |
| `isn't just X — it's Y` | The em-dash reversal | Make the positive claim once. |
| Triples | "Diagnose. Design. Deliver." | Cadence is not an argument. Keep one if it is genuinely a sequence the buyer follows. |

The underlying rule: **do not announce the quality, demonstrate it.** A page
that says it avoids jargon while using jargon has spent its credibility on the
claim. A page that quietly uses the buyer's words does not need the claim.

## Page order

From Wynter's B2B message-layers framework, in this order:

1. **Clarity** — what is this?
2. **Relevance** — is it for me?
3. **Value** — do I want the outcome?
4. **Differentiation** — why this company?
5. **Friction** — what is the next step, and how small is it?

Most of our old pages opened at layer four. A visitor who does not know what
we do cannot evaluate why we are different.

## Naming services

Name the situation the buyer is in, not the discipline we practise.

| Do not write | Write |
| --- | --- |
| Data Health & Analytics Readiness | You have dashboards. You do not trust the numbers. |
| AI Readiness & Automation Opportunity Assessment | You are being told to adopt AI and nobody can say where it helps. |
| Cloud & DevOps Readiness Review | Releases hurt, and nobody is sure why. |

The formal engagement name can still exist for the contract. It should not be
the first thing on the page.

## Evidence

Case studies follow one structure:

**Problem → what was at stake → what we did → what changed → the evidence.**

Rules for numbers:

- Only publish a number that can be substantiated. No invented percentages, no
  invented client counts, no "up to" ranges built backwards from a claim.
- If the client cannot be named, name the industry and the scale.
- If the outcome cannot be measured, say what was delivered instead of implying
  a result.
- Where we do not know, write that we do not know.

## Maturity labels

Products carry their real state: `open source`, `alpha`, `experiment`,
`research`, `live`. An experiment never gets described in the register of a
supported product, and a roadmap item is never written in the present tense.
